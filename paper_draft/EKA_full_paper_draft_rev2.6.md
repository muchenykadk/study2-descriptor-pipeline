**Abstract.** Demolition concrete is typically downcycled into aggregate, discarding the structural and material value of its irregular fragments. Their singular geometry and surface traces are design-relevant, yet assessing them has relied on tacit, fragment-by-fragment judgement that cannot scale. This paper develops a computational basis for characterizing such fragments as queryable design data through two sequential practice-led studies. Study 1, a built public commission in urban furniture, demonstrated the design potential of irregular concrete and yielded an information requirements scheme derived from its documented gaps. Study 2 prototypes a pipeline that takes a 3D scan of a fragment, extracts geometric descriptors deterministically, classifies surface conditions with a vision-language model, and consolidates the result into a queryable per-fragment record. No existing dataset covers this material, so the study builds and releases its own ground truth: twelve scanned fragments and twenty-six hand-labelled surface tiles with the labelling protocol. Scored on that set against a null baseline, the surface classifier does not exceed it, and two findings that reach beyond concrete account for the ceiling: capture resolution fixes which categories are observable before any model is chosen, and multi-label prompting suppresses the distinctive features that carry design information. The contribution is a descriptor schema grounded in built practice, a verified geometric characterization pipeline, a method for deriving a taxonomy from what the capture resolves, and the released fragment and tile set on which all of it was measured.

**Keywords:** concrete reuse, irregular material, computational characterization, design-to-fabrication, material-adaptive design [confirm keyword list with EKA template]

## 1. Introduction

Construction and demolition (C&D) waste accounts for roughly one-third of solid waste in the European Union, over 800 million tonnes annually (European Environment Agency, 2020). Although concrete recycling rates are high, the material is primarily downcycled into low-grade aggregates through an energy-intensive crushing process, discarding its original structural capacity (European Commission, 2023). Volumes of deconstruction concrete are expected to grow across Europe as successive generations of building stock reach the end of their service lives (Wiedenhofer et al., 2015). Underlying this downcycling is a framing problem: demolition waste is treated as a bulk flow to be processed, not as discrete fragments with differentiated properties and architectural potential.

Composing a spatial installation from variable, non-standard elements curated by their individual features is a long-standing architectural and artistic practice. In traditional Chinese gardens, lake stones of irregular shape are arranged into rockeries, garden walls, and stairs (Wang et al., 2024); salvaged material has likewise been reinterpreted in modern work, from the Watts Towers that Simon Rodia built from waste [CITATION NEEDED: Watts Towers scholarly source] to Wang Shu's facades of recycled brick and tile at the Xiangshan campus [CITATION NEEDED: Amateur Architecture Studio / wa pan source]. Treating reclaimed concrete in this spirit, whether components from planned dismantling or fragments from demolition, exposes an information asymmetry. Traditional material knowledge reaches designers in two modes: through direct, hands-on study of material character, as taught in the Bauhaus preliminary course (Itten, 1975; Moholy-Nagy, 1938), or through selection from pre-characterized catalogues, where standardization guarantees the link between on-paper design and built result (Ashby & Johnson, 2002). A reclaimed concrete fragment resists both modes: as a found condition it is too singular for any catalogue, while a stock of hundreds of unique, heavy pieces exceeds what hands-on study can cover. Appearance, moreover, is only half of what must be known. For material this heavy, irregular, and non-malleable, handling machinery, connection feasibility, and placement constraints are not downstream logistics but coupled design decisions (Widmer et al., 2023; Küpfer et al., 2024; Bertola et al., 2025). Designing with demolition concrete at scale therefore depends on enriched, queryable per-fragment models, spanning appearance, geometry, and procedure, available where design decisions are made.

Existing methods each address one dimension of this need, largely in isolation from the others. On the documentation side, material passports and reuse databases record component-level data (Honic et al., 2021; Luo et al., 2024), and recent work structures pre-demolition audits into knowledge graphs queryable in natural language [KG-audit CITATION NEEDED]; yet what they hold (element types, quantities, coarse condition) remains at document granularity and does not actively enter computational design processes. On the design side, research on reclaimed inventories concentrates on geometric stock matching and allocation, treating elements as geometric tokens to fit a target form (Cousin et al., 2023; Eschenbach et al., 2023; Skoury et al., 2024; Blum et al., 2025); some of this work extends the allocation objective to environmental accounting, incorporating life cycle assessment and embodied carbon for reclaimed steel (Brütting et al., 2020) and cut concrete blocks (Önalan et al., 2025). Across these approaches the element model remains geometric and quantitative: surface and condition stay outside it, leaving each element's singularity unexplored as design data. For surface characterization itself, construction-inspection computer vision detects defects reliably (Dorafshan et al., 2018; Niklaus et al., 2023) but over closed categories that cannot describe the open-ended vocabulary of demolition surfaces. Vision-language models lift this restriction, classifying against any user-defined taxonomy without retraining (OpenAI, 2024), yet stop at image-level labels that never locate a condition on the geometry governing its structural role. Workflows that do operationalize the appearance of found objects computationally, from digitized rockery stones (Wang et al., 2024) to vision-based matching of scanned industrial scrap (Certain Measures, 2016), remain small-scale and object-specific. Semantic frameworks could bind such characterization into queryable knowledge (Rossi et al., 2026; Elshani et al., 2025; Elshani et al., 2024), but presuppose properties already recorded elsewhere. No existing pipeline takes scan geometry and vision-based surface classification as primary input and integrates open-vocabulary classification, spatial localization, morphological analysis, and inventory structuring for demolition concrete.

This paper addresses the gap through a practice-led trajectory of two sequential studies. Design explorations with irregular rubble have so far concentrated on wall-type structural assemblies and geometric matching, largely as laboratory prototypes (Grangeot et al., 2024; Oreb et al., 2024; Lu et al., 2026). Study 1, a built public commission, carried the material instead into an urban furniture project in active public use, demonstrating both the potential and the bottleneck of upcycling feature-rich, irregular rubble. Traces of past use, such as cladding residues, formwork imprints, and remaining pipe openings, acted as design-relevant properties that added character to the built work, an approach rooted in the spolia tradition; geometry and surface condition jointly determined each fragment's structural and aesthetic assignment [Study 1 / IASS CITATION NEEDED]. The characterization behind these decisions, however, relied on fragment-by-fragment designer judgement and left no computable record. The gaps this surfaced motivate Study 2, which prototypes a computational pipeline that extracts the required data from 3D scans: deterministic computation for geometric descriptors, vision-model classification for surface features. The extracted descriptors, together with registry data, are structured under a schema derived from Study 1's information requirements and linked onward to encoded domain knowledge of handling and design implications. The pipeline is demonstrated on real fragment scans from Study 1 and assessed retrospectively against that project's documented design decisions, evaluating whether computed characterization can recover the selection logic the designer exercised through direct material knowledge. Within the scope of this paper the records are structured for querying but not yet coupled to a live design workflow.

## 2. Objectives and Methodology

### 2.1 Research objectives

The overarching aim of this research is an interactive design environment for reclaimed concrete, in which enriched, queryable per-fragment models inform the design phase directly. Within that aim, this paper pursues three objectives:

1. **Foundation.** To demonstrate the design and aesthetic potential of irregular concrete fragments through a built public commission in active public use, and to derive an information requirements scheme from its documented gaps.

2. **Instrument.** To prototype a computational pipeline that extracts geometric descriptors from 3D scans, adds a surface-descriptor layer whose reliability is measured and bounded, and structures both, with registry data, under a schema derived from the Study 1 requirements.

3. **Evaluation.** To demonstrate the pipeline on real Study 1 scans, with attributes that resist scan-based measurement carried as flagged provisional entries, to establish what the capture itself makes observable, and to evaluate the computed descriptors retrospectively against the documented design decisions of Study 1.

### 2.2 Methodology

The research adopts a practice-led design in which making is the mode of inquiry. Study 1 is a Research through Design case study (Frayling, 1993): a live public commission is executed as the research instrument, so that the act of designing and building produces the knowledge sought. Such knowledge is situated and largely tacit, and a practice-led inquiry that ends at one built work leaves it bound to that work. The methodological task of Study 2 is therefore externalization: turning the situated characterization into a computational instrument that can be applied, queried, and reused beyond the original project.

Because requirements, material, and design decisions all derive from one documented project, the instrument can be evaluated retrospectively against it. Each documented decision is re-expressed as a query over the computed descriptors, and agreement or divergence is read as evidence of what the characterization captures and what remains tacit. This offers first evidence of design relevance within a single case, made by the same designers who conducted the work; it is not independent validation.

## 3. Study 1: Requirements from Built Practice

Study 1 built an urban furniture commission from demolition concrete rubble as a Research through Design case study, producing two prototypes now in active public use. Around twenty cast-in-place fragments were selected from the deconstruction site of an early twentieth-century building and digitized by photogrammetry. A digital-physical workflow (Fig. 1) carried the project from this fragment inventory through a hybrid manual and computational design stage with rigid-body stability simulation, on-site concrete assembly guided by augmented-reality placement, and final assembly with adaptively fabricated timber connections. The stage-by-stage implementation and full findings are reported in [IASS CITATION NEEDED].

One design principle of the built work was to carry the modern spolia spirit: treating the singular attributes of the rubble as design material. Former pipe penetrations were reinterpreted as integrated planters, cast faces and weathering were retained as exposed surfaces, and the contrast between worn concrete and new timber kept the reuse visible. Surface character thus informed fragment selection and placement together with geometry. This is the potential the case study demonstrated: irregular rubble, read for its material history, can hold both aesthetic and structural roles in a public landscape setting. The decisions concerning aesthetic features, however, remained a manual curation process that does not scale beyond a small set.

A review of the project implementation records identified the information required at each stage, mapped in Fig. 1; the full gap analysis is given in [IASS CITATION NEEDED]. The required information spans three dimensions. Structurally: estimated volume and mass, centre of mass, planar surfaces, exposed reinforcement, and crack grading. Aesthetically: colour, surface roughness or aggregate size, and categorized surface features such as formwork imprints, tile residue, and weathering. Procedurally: machinery matched to a fragment's mass class and connections feasible for a given interface condition. These requirements define the descriptor schema developed in the following section.

The eleven surface features are grouped in the schema by how they arise, as formation, composition, inclusion and colour, and that grouping cuts across Fig. 1's structural, aesthetic and procedural tracks. `rebar_visible` is an inclusion by appearance and a structural requirement by use, which is why it drives drilling guidance and not surface finishing.

Their provenance against the two published concrete-defect benchmarks inverts the expected pattern. Of the eleven active features, one is adopted directly from a benchmarked class, three are related to one without being equivalent, and seven are project-defined. Of the six retired features, five had direct benchmark correspondence: `crack`, `spalling`, `efflorescence`, `discolouration` and `weathered` are all classes in CODEBRIM (Mundt et al., 2019) or dacl10k (Flotzinger et al., 2024), and all were retired here on evidence. The categories the field has labelled data for are close to being the categories this capture cannot support, while the conditions that carry design information on demolition debris, how a piece was cast, how it was broken and what was cast into it, appear in no benchmark. The vocabulary is therefore specific to this stock. The procedure that produced it is not.

Two things follow from the status column and are taken up in Section 5.1. The requirements met with `measured` values are exactly those a geometric computation can reach, and `handling_class` and `connection_strategy` reach `derived` without consulting a surface label at all. Everything that depends on the vision layer carries `provisional` at best.

## 4. Study 2: Fragment Characterization Pipeline

Study 2 responds to the gaps identified in Study 1 by developing a prototype computational pipeline. It runs on one fragment at a time and produces one queryable record, enriching the 3D scan input with extracted geometric and surface descriptors and their procedural and design implications. Fig. 2 traces the dataflow across the four stages below.

**Preparation.** The pipeline takes a textured mesh from photogrammetry and prepares it through remeshing at 2 mm, UV unwrapping and texture rebaking, a process semi-automated by a custom Blender script, producing a GLB file paired with a texture image baked onto the new UV layout. Because a concrete piece is scanned resting on the ground, its contact face is never captured and is reconstructed as patched geometry. Those patched faces are marked at this stage, so that later steps exclude them from feature extraction and flag any plane sharing their orientation as unreliable.

**Descriptor extraction.** Two types of descriptor are extracted by different methods. Geometric descriptors are computed deterministically from the mesh, including principal dimensions, volume, a convexity ratio and an estimated mass; RANSAC segmentation returns a set of planes, each with an area, orientation and fit deviation; multi-scale curvature quantifies surface coarseness. To extract surface descriptors the mesh is divided into regions, so that a label can be attached to one patch of surface. Regions are built from the fitted planes, with the remaining surface grouped by adjacency; the division is geometric and cannot see the texture, so one region may carry several features. Each region's crop is cut from the texture through the UV coordinates and classified by a vision-language model (GPT-4o used in the prototype) against a configurable feature list, calibrated with labelled example images. Each image is classified three times, and a feature is kept when a majority of runs report it. Values are stored at three levels: the whole fragment, each fitted plane, and each region.

**Derivation.** Encoded rules map descriptors into processing and construction actions and into design implications, each rule pairing a set of conditions with an outcome, and each outcome stored with the rule that fired and the descriptor values that triggered it. A fragment, meaning the whole piece of rubble, receives one handling class, from its mass and longest dimension. Each face receives one connection strategy, from its flatness, area and scan reliability, and one design assignment, from its surface features. In every case the first matching rule decides. Candidate uses work differently: each checks the whole piece, by mass, thickness or height, and at least one of its faces, by area, flatness and feature. A seat block, for example, needs both a sittable height and a flat enough top face. Every use is checked, so a fragment may be proposed for several or for none. In this prototype the thresholds come from Study 1 practice and the reuse literature and await expert review. Table 2 gives the complete rule set.

**Presentation and query.** Each fragment is saved as one JSON file and rendered as an HTML page with a 3D viewer, where features are highlighted on the model and the measured and derived values appear on hover; an index page lists the set. Across records, a command-line tool selects fragments by use, feature, face area, flatness, mass, thickness, handling class, connection strategy or design assignment, and ranks the results. A query the descriptors cannot answer fails openly instead of returning a plausible wrong result, and the surface descriptors can be excluded, so a selection made on geometry alone can be compared with one that uses them. Both interfaces are provisional. Because every descriptor is an explicit value, a design environment could read the same records directly, and the same values would map without transformation into a linked-data representation if relations between fragments were later needed.

What the scan can show bounds what can be searched for. A category was admitted to the vocabulary only where its evidence is larger than the texture resolves, which reduced sixteen candidates to eleven. Cracks are the instructive loss: they matter for reuse and they are not legible at this resolution, so they are not offered as something to select on. Naming that limit serves a designer better than a category that returns answers nobody can trust.

A fragment is described in two overlapping ways, because two design questions need different units. A plane answers whether a surface can bear weight or take a fixing. A region answers what a surface is made of. The two do not correspond one to one, so a fragment may hold a large flat plane whose surface was never described, or a well-described patch that no plane covers. Section 5.1 reports how far they diverge.

## 5. Results and Evaluation

### 5.1 Pipeline results and validation on twelve fragments

Twelve fragments were processed end to end. The field offers no annotated dataset of scanned demolition fragments (Section 7), so every reference value used here was produced inside the study. That shaped the evaluation: with ground truth this scarce, the useful question is which claims the evidence can support.

**Geometric descriptors are verified.** Oriented bounding box, volume, and mass were recomputed independently from the source mesh for three fragments spanning the size range (FS-005, FS-010, FS-012) and match the stored records exactly. Density is assumed at 2400 kg/m³, so mass carries that assumption and is flagged provisional; handling class traces to the measured maximum dimension and to that mass. These three quantities are deterministic and reproduce across runs, and they carry the retrospective evaluation in Section 5.2. The plane search is separate: RANSAC draws from a global random source, and three runs of FS-002 on identical input returned first-plane areas of 0.9995, 1.1011 and 1.0909 m². Planar area, orientation, and the region identifiers built from them are reproducible only once that source is seeded, which is a small implementation detail with a large consequence, since shifting region boundaries also shift what each classification call is asked about.

**Capture resolution governs everything else.** Texel density, the number of atlas pixels per millimetre of real fragment surface, is computed as √(*A*<sub>uv</sub> · *S*² / *A*<sub>mm</sub>), where *A*<sub>uv</sub> is the fraction of the atlas carrying UV, *S* the atlas side, and *A*<sub>mm</sub> the mesh surface area. It is set in the texture bake, a step usually treated as rendering, and it fixes what any later method can see. Raising the bake resolution from 1080 to 4096 and reducing the UV island margin from 0.02 to 0.002 of the atlas moved the fragment set from about 0.24 to 1.27–2.51 px/mm. The remaining spread across the eleven usable fragments comes from fragment size, one 4096 sheet covering 1.2 m² against 5.0 m², so detectability varies from piece to piece and any per-feature rate depends on which fragment a region came from. One fragment sits at 0.20 px/mm because its source predates the current capture protocol; it is excluded from all texture-based claims and stays in the geometric set.

**The taxonomy follows that measurement, and the ground truth was sampled blind.** The admission test of Section 4 reduced sixteen candidate categories to eleven, and it explains why the standard inspection vocabularies transfer only in part: ACI 201.1R, EN 1504, CODEBRIM (Mundt et al., 2019), and dacl10k (Flotzinger et al., 2024) all assume in-service structures photographed at close range, and demolition debris under handheld photogrammetry meets neither condition. Calibration works within the same limits, sending labelled exemplar crops from this same building ahead of the regions, so that samples of the material define each category. For testing, thirty-nine tiles of about 250 mm of real surface were taken at random positions across the twelve fragments and labelled by hand. Random sampling was deliberate: tiles chosen for showing clear features would measure the best case and report it as the average. Thirteen (33%) were unreadable through smear, blur, or empty atlas, leaving twenty-six. That exclusion rate is itself a measurement of the capture, and a second coverage figure sits beside it. Across the eleven textured fragments the pipeline segmented 134 surface regions and classified 56 of them, 42%, holding back 52% of segmented area: 41 regions on sparse UV, 30 on fragmented UV, and 7 on unusable texture. Both figures belong in the result, because a classification rate on this material means little without the share of surface that could be classified at all.

**The classifier matches a null baseline and does not exceed it.** The comparison that decides whether the vision layer contributes anything is a model that looks at nothing and always answers the two commonest features, `broken_face, exposed_aggregate`:

| | recall | precision | exact match |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| null model | 80% | **75%** | 11/26 |
| multi-label classifier | 80% | **66%** | 10/26 |
| one binary question per feature | 69% | **75%** | — |

A second reading points the same way. `broken_face` is a statement about how a surface was formed, so it should track the geometry of the region it is applied to. It appears on 98% of non-planar regions and on 93% of planar ones, and `exposed_aggregate` on 95% and 93%. Neither label distinguishes a flat surface from a rough one, which is what a formation label exists to do.

Per feature, the two frequent categories are recovered at rates a constant guess already achieves (`broken_face` 95% recall at 80% precision, `exposed_aggregate` 100% at 72%), and every distinctive category fails under multi-label prompting: `brick_inclusion` 0 of 5, `formwork_face` 0 of 2, `rebar_visible` and `pipe_opening` 0 of 1 each. Twenty-five of twenty-six tiles received one of two answers. The result held through a taxonomy rebuilt on observability, exemplar calibration, three-run voting, reduced batch size, and five prompt revisions, which marks it as structural.

**Question framing changes what is recovered.** Asking one yes/no question per feature on the identical images moved `brick_inclusion` from 0 of 5 to 2 of 5, with one false positive, and made `exposed_aggregate` discriminate: recall fell from 100% to 61% while precision rose from 72% to 92%. The multi-label prompt offers eleven categories at once, so naming the two commonest is a safe answer; a direct question removes that option. One feature moved, on five instances, so binary framing is a lead and not a fix. The finding is that multi-label prompting suppresses exactly the distinctive features that carry design information.

**The full run reproduces that pattern, and adds a disagreement inside the pipeline.** Of the 59 classified regions, `broken_face` was reported on 58 and `exposed_aggregate` on 54, both across all eleven fragments. Every distinctive category is rare: `brick_inclusion` on 5 regions across 4 fragments, `paste_dominant` on 5 regions of one fragment, `tile_remnant` on 2, `pipe_opening` and `formwork_face` on 1 each. Reinforcement was reported on no region at all. The whole-atlas pass, which puts one image of the entire fragment to the same model against the same vocabulary, disagrees: it names `rebar_visible` on 4 of 11 fragments, `tile_remnant` on 3, and `formwork_face` on 2. Two views of the same fragment, differing only in framing, return different answers on the one category with a safety consequence, and neither has ground truth behind it. Section 6.2 takes this up.

**The design-factor layer runs, and on this corpus it barely varies.** Every one of the eleven fragments returns `excavator` for handling class, because every one exceeds the 800 mm dimension threshold and the mass rules are never reached. Connection strategy returns `direct_bolt` on 68 of 82 faces, with 10 falling to gravity and 4 to an adaptive bracket. Design assignment returns `unassigned` on 63 of 82, and `show_face` exactly once across the whole corpus. The rules are traceable and reproduce exactly, and they discriminate very little here. Two causes separate cleanly and should not be conflated. The handling result is a property of the sample: the fragments were selected in Study 1 for a landscape installation, so all are large slab pieces from one building, and a factor that would separate a 20 kg piece from a 2000 kg one has nothing to separate. The assignment result has a different cause, and it is structural. Segmentation produces two kinds of region, the flat areas found by plane fitting and the non-planar remainder, and only the flat ones have a corresponding entry in the face record. Of the 57 regions classified across the corpus, 43 are clusters and 14 are planes, so three quarters of the successful classifications have no face to attach to and are discarded before the design rules run. Fourteen of 82 faces carry a surface label; the remaining 68 are unlabelled, which is why the assignment defaults. Improving the classifier would change little here, because most of its output has nowhere to go. The first result says the corpus cannot demonstrate the handling factor. The second says the surface-driven factors are starved of input by the schema itself, independently of how well the classification performs.

**One measured value does not mean what the rule using it assumes.** A face's area is estimated by projecting its plane inliers and taking their convex hull, so it reports the extent over which the plane holds and not the area of a surface a hand could rest on. On FS-002 the three largest faces, at 1.09, 1.02 and 0.78 m², have no connected patch of surface behind them at all, and the flattest of the eight is among them. `connection_strategy` nonetheless tests that figure against a 0.1 m² minimum to decide whether a fixing plate can be seated, and returns `direct_bolt` on 68 of 82 faces across the corpus. The rule and the measurement are not describing the same thing, and the connection result should be read with that in mind until the area is recomputed from contiguous surface.

**Repeatability is high, and it measures stability.** Across three runs per region, 98% of feature observations were unanimous. The companion figure explains that agreement: 82% of regions returned the identical pair of features, and three runs of a near-constant output will agree.

**Localization was withdrawn on evidence.** The prototype originally required a bounding box for each detected feature. An audit found all 68 coordinates to be exact multiples of ten, seventeen detections drawn from seven distinct boxes, and the dead-centre box [40,40,60,60] returned six times across unrelated fragments and features; three of the boxes checked pointed mostly at masked-out non-material. The schema shares the fault, since it demanded a coordinate and offered no way to decline. Localization is therefore switched off, and the checks that would police it are built and kept: a null answer is permitted and preferred, a candidate box must fall at least half on real material, stock and centred coordinates are rejected, and a box must agree across runs. Those checks await live output, so this paper places no surface feature at a position on the fragment.

### 5.2 Retrospective evaluation against Study 1 decisions

Seven decisions from Study 1 were reconstructed from project records and assigned to four query competencies after Mitropoulou et al. (2026): attribute filtering, multi-criteria selection, a condition located on a named face, and decisions the descriptors cannot state at all. Each was encoded as a query over the frozen records and run twice, once on the full record and once with the surface descriptors withheld. No threshold was chosen for this evaluation. Every query cites a rule already present in the factor configuration, fixed before the corpus was processed, so the criteria and the thresholds both predate the result. The decision ledger and the query encodings are in the project data.

Results are reported as the size of the returned set together with recall against it. Precision would need a confirmed negative set, and the ledger records the decisions that were built rather than the pieces rejected, so it holds positives and almost no negatives. A query returning everything scores full recall, which is why the set size carries the meaning.

| Decision | Set returned | Recall | Geometry only |
|---|---|---|---|
| Leaning back-rest | 12 of 12 | 1 of 1 | unchanged |
| Stable base | 7 of 12 | 3 of 3 | unchanged |
| Timber connection face | 68 of 87 faces | 0.88 | unchanged |
| Carrier for a seating platform | 4 of 12 | 1 of 2 | unchanged |
| Show face | 5 of 12 | 2 of 3 | 0 of 12 |
| Pipe penetration as planter | 2 of 12 | 1 of 2 | 0 of 12 |

Four of six queries narrowed the field while keeping part of the documented answer. Two depend on the surface descriptors and return nothing without them; the other four are answered from geometry alone on this corpus. Two rows fail. The leaning back-rest returns every fragment, because its thresholds of 0.2 m² of face at 15 mm flatness and 150 kg are cleared by all twelve, the smallest face being 0.32 m² at 1.8 mm and the lightest fragment 181 kg. The connection face performs worse than not filtering: it removes 22% of the candidates and loses 12% of the documented answers doing so.

Each failure has a separate and identifiable cause, and none of them is a shortage of data.

**Thresholds set below the range the corpus occupies.** The leaning rule asks for a face of 0.2 m² at 15 mm flatness on a fragment of 150 kg, and every fragment clears all three by a wide margin. Handling class turns on a longest dimension of 800 mm, and the smallest fragment is 957 mm. Both rules are sound and neither divides this material. Tightening them against a known answer would fit the test to the result, so they are reported as ranks instead.

**A descriptor whose meaning the rules misread.** Face area is the convex hull of the plane inliers. The connection rule reads it as a contiguous bearing area and returns `direct_bolt` on 68 of 87 faces, including six of eight on each of the two fragments recorded as taking no connection. Against a null query returning every face, it reaches lower recall at equal precision.

**A rule schema demanding flatness where the function does not need it.** Three of the eleven candidate uses ask only about surface character and specify no flatness at all, yet were evaluated over planar faces alone. The clearest case asks for a broken face and exposed aggregate and could not fire on a broken face. Allowing those three to read the region the model was actually shown, while the eight load-bearing uses continue to require a plane, raised show-face recall from one of three to two of three and made the planter reuse expressible for the first time. The classification is unchanged; only where the rule looks for it has changed.

**A rule vocabulary written for surfaces the material does not have.** Features are classified on surface regions, and design rules are written over planar faces. Roughly half of each fragment segments into irregular clusters rather than planes, and that half is also the legible half: cluster regions are classified at 75% against 23% for planar ones, whose inliers scatter across the texture atlas and fail the coverage gate. Of 65 successful classifications, 49 sit on non-planar regions and reach no rule. On FS-002 the model reports `pipe_opening` correctly on the region that carries it; no planar face holds the label, and the planter rule, which requires one, cannot fire.

Attaching those classifications to the nearest planar face was implemented and rejected. Segmentation already assigns every triangle within 8 mm and 30° of a plane to that plane, so a cluster is by construction the surface that is not planar, and the clusters measure 27 to 323 mm from their nearest plane at 35° to 141°. Attaching them would record a condition on a face that does not have it.

The first three were repaired and the table above reports the state after repair. The fourth remains, and it is not a fault in the record. Eight of the eleven candidate uses put a load or a body on a surface, so flatness is their function and a plane is genuinely required. Those are the uses that cannot reach the half of the material that reads most reliably. Fracture surfaces are what distinguish demolition concrete, they are where the classifier works best, and a vocabulary of planar bearing faces has nothing to propose for them.

This is proof-of-principle within a single project, and the decisions serving as ground truth were made by the same designers who conducted the research. It is first evidence of design relevance, not independent validation.

## 6. Discussion and Outlook

### 6.1 Position against existing approaches

The demonstrated pipeline addresses what the approaches reviewed in Section 1 leave open. Stock-matching and allocation workflows (Cousin et al., 2023; Eschenbach et al., 2023) operate on element models that are geometric and quantitative; the per-fragment record demonstrated here extends that element model with classified surface conditions bound to the geometry that carries them, so that the aesthetic and material-history properties which drove the Study 1 decisions become part of the same computational object as dimensions and planarity. Passport and audit systems (Honic et al., 2021) record at document granularity what this pipeline computes at fragment granularity from the scan itself, requiring no pre-existing documentation, which is the prevailing condition for demolition rubble. Open-vocabulary classification bound to surface regions likewise addresses what inspection-oriented computer vision leaves out, whose closed defect categories and image-level outputs (Dorafshan et al., 2018) do not serve design queries, although Section 5.1 shows that a better framing leaves the task itself open. What the pipeline does not yet do, integration into a live design environment and validated procedural knowledge, is bounded in Section 6.4.

### 6.2 Vision-language models for material characterization

Building the prototype produced four measured findings about vision-language models on this task, each of which applies beyond concrete.

*The unit of classification should follow the material.* Classification over a uniform texture grid, an obvious first architecture, fails for a structural reason: the model judges each submitted image once and repeats that judgement across its cells, so nominally per-cell output collapses to per-image output, and independent judgements of adjacent tiles draw label boundaries across continuous surfaces where none exist. Segmenting into coherent surface regions first matches the material structure of demolition fragments and gives the model one whole surface with context. The same mechanism recurs at fragment scale: sending a whole fragment in one request let the model compare regions against one another and settle on a house style, returning an identical feature set for every image in four of seven calls, which small independent batches correct.

*Question framing is a variable of the method.* Three framings of the same question, on the same material and the same vocabulary, give three answers. Offering eleven categories at once produced the two commonest almost everywhere. One direct yes/no question per category recovered a distinctive inclusion that framing missed. Putting one image of the whole fragment instead of one image per surface region returned exposed reinforcement on four fragments where the region-level pass returned it on none (Section 5.1). Naming the frequent categories is a safe answer, and the framings that remove that safety are the ones that surface the rare categories, at the cost of a false-positive rate nobody here can measure. Studies reporting open-vocabulary classification rates on material surfaces should therefore state their framing as part of the method and report a null baseline, since answering the two commonest features unconditionally accounts for the whole of the 80% recall seen here.

*A required field will be filled.* Obliged to return a bounding box with no option to decline, the model produced stock coordinates (Section 5.1). Read as invention, this points the remedy at the model; read as a schema that ruled out "I cannot tell", it points at the interface, which is where the fix belongs. The transferable rule is that any field a model must return needs an explicit null option and an independent validity check, otherwise a placeholder and a real answer look alike.

*Capture resolution bounds the achievable vocabulary.* Which categories are recoverable is settled before any model is chosen, by parameters set in the texture bake. The resulting procedure transfers easily: measure the resolution first, admit only categories whose evidence exceeds the sampling window, and record the retired ones with their reasons, so that the vocabulary can be checked against the capture that produced it.

### 6.3 Design implications

Making these descriptors computable and queryable changes how design with reclaimed concrete can proceed. A designer can interrogate a fragment stock by geometry, surface character, and procedural constraint together, so selection and assignment become reproducible, multi-criteria queries, no longer bound to one person's memory of the pile. Aesthetic and material-history properties, previously accessible only by handling each piece, become searchable, allowing the spolia sensibility of Study 1 to operate at the scale of a demolition stock. Bringing handling and connection constraints into the same query surface lets them shape design decisions at the point those decisions are made. At scale, and kept under tracking within a digital-twin system, this could support the feature-curated assembly of variable elements that traditions such as the Chinese garden achieved by hand (Wang et al., 2024).

### 6.4 Limitations

The evaluation basis is small and single-case (Section 2.2), and Section 5.1 puts numbers on its limits. The surface-descriptor layer matches a null baseline, so the paper claims no accuracy for it, and it stays silent on the four categories that rest on one or two labelled instances, where a zero records absent evidence. One person both selected the calibration exemplars and labelled the test tiles, which is the strongest single caveat on the numbers and follows directly from working without an external dataset. Two design factors that depend on the surface layer, drill zone and finishing requirement, are computed and withheld. Both read exposed reinforcement from the region labels, where it has never been reported, while the whole-atlas pass reports it on four fragments. Drilling guidance resting on a negative that the pipeline contradicts elsewhere in the same record has no place in a published output. The remaining procedural thresholds are executed and traceable, await expert review, and carry the data status `proposed`. Concrete strength, reinforcement layout, and centre of mass resist scan-based measurement and remain assumptions, flagged as provisional entries. One fragment is excluded from all texture-based claims because its source predates the current capture protocol, and detectability across the remaining eleven varies by a factor of two with fragment size, so per-feature rates depend on which fragment a region came from. An external benchmark remains necessary before automated cataloguing of surface features can be relied upon, particularly for structurally consequential conditions such as exposed reinforcement and cracking; Section 7 discusses what stands in the way of building one.

### 6.5 Outlook and future work

Several directions extend the work. Descriptor combinations could inform higher-order and sequential behaviours, such as assembly order and rules where one fragment's placement constrains the next, which a per-fragment record does not yet express. Three nearer-term steps follow from Section 5.1, each small enough to complete quickly. Classification could run on the source scan atlas, which carries roughly twice the texel density and is free of the directional smear, to test whether the ceiling found here belongs to the material or to the texture bake. That step is also the precondition for measuring cracks, since a line filter needs the width the bake removes, and it would return the lengths and orientations that crack grading actually asks for. The binary framing could be applied across the whole vocabulary on a larger tile set, to settle whether that result generalizes. Recovering part of the region area currently declined would enlarge the evidence base with the scans already in hand. Grounding the procedural knowledge in expert input, adding a resting-pose estimate, and expanding the fragment set remain open. Beyond the demonstrated case, the same pipeline is anticipated for documented and standing stock: applied to an element scheduled for deconstruction, its descriptors could enrich a pre-demolition audit, with the scan-reliability machinery making the partial-coverage scans of standing elements usable.

## 7. Conclusion

Study 1, a built public commission, established the design potential of feature-rich demolition rubble and derived the information requirements its reuse demands. Study 2 turned those requirements into a working pipeline that extracts descriptors from 3D scans and consolidates them, with surface conditions bound to the geometry, into a queryable per-fragment record. Its geometric half is verified and reproducible, and it carries the design-relevance evaluation. Its surface half was measured against ground truth built for the purpose and matches a null baseline. Building the prototype far enough to establish that is itself a result, and it yielded three findings that hold beyond this material: capture resolution fixes which categories are observable before any model is chosen, question framing changes what a vision-language model reports on identical images, and a schema that requires an answer will receive a placeholder. The two studies together provide the schema, the instrument, and a measured account of where it stops, bounded as described in Section 6.4.

A constraint on that work lies in the data available to the field. The benchmark datasets that support machine learning on concrete condition are two-dimensional and were photographed on in-service structures at close range (Mundt et al., 2019; Flotzinger et al., 2024), so their categories and their image quality both assume conditions that demolition debris does not meet. The published three-dimensional construction datasets record whole buildings and active sites, not the detached irregular fragments that selective demolition produces (Kaufmann et al., 2025). A search of the published datasets found none of three-dimensionally scanned demolition fragments carrying annotated surface features, so any evaluation of surface classification on this material must supply its own ground truth, as Section 5.1 did. The twelve fragments documented here, together with the twenty-six randomly sampled and hand-labelled surface tiles and the labelling protocol used to produce them, are released as a first contribution toward such a dataset.
